## Choose your way into the room

You can arrive with an image, a stalled draft, a book, or a specific reader’s request. Those need different conversations.

The short version remains `story-run`: one coordinating instruction carries the agreed project through development, writing, review, repair and selected delivery. The deeper skill is knowing which decision deserves attention now. You can use the whole process or enter at the stage your work actually needs.

| What you bring | The useful first move | What to protect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A feeling or loose idea | Explore distinct possible movies before choosing an engine. Use story-run or story-office-hours. | The image, relationship or experience that made you care; an unfinished idea is not permission to impose a generic plot. |
| A finished or partial screenplay | Read it, reconstruct its actual design, then diagnose. Use story-studio or ask story-run to begin from the draft. | Your original, its voice, ending and choices you have explicitly reserved. |
| A novel, memoir, play, article or recording | Preserve and read the source, agree fidelity and permissions status, then identify a cinematic version. Use story-adapt. | The source and its locations; distinguish what was read from what is missing. |
| A meeting or submission request | Define the reader and exact deliverable. Make only the relevant page or package. | Length, required fields, actual claims and the reader’s decision; internal analysis should not spill into the submission. |

Claude Code

```text
/story-run My idea is [your idea]. Create a separate feature project. Take it through a complete screenplay, full-draft diagnosis, meaningful revision, current quality checks, the studio materials I choose, and built, inspected PDFs with editable sources. Ask me about important creative choices. Save progress and do not send or publish my work.
```

Codex

```text
$story-run My idea is [your idea]. Create a separate feature project. Take it through a complete screenplay, full-draft diagnosis, meaningful revision, current quality checks, the studio materials I choose, and built, inspected PDFs with editable sources. Ask me about important creative choices. Save progress and do not send or publish my work.
```

> The command examples are messages to your writing assistant, not terminal commands. Use the Claude Code or Codex line for your app, then continue the conversation in ordinary language.

Add the full package if you want all 15 studio sections and all 16 pitches. Or say “screenplay and synopsis only.” Give creative constraints in ordinary language: “Keep the ending unresolved,” “Dry humor without everyone sounding witty,” or “Ask before adding locations.” Scope is a creative decision, not a technical preference.

## What happens between the idea and the files

Each stage should earn the next. The assistant handles the organization; you should be able to understand the decision it is making.

### Discover the particular movie

**Purpose:** Find the person, contradiction, encounter or audience experience inside the seed. Compare a few genuinely different interpretations if the idea is loose.

**Decision:** Which version do you want to spend a film watching, and what must survive development?

**Record:** A brief, alternatives, chosen direction, protected constraints and unresolved questions.

**Check:** Can we explain why this version is specific, rather than a genre label plus a mood?

### Research the source promise when relevant

**Purpose:** For based-on or inspired-by work, investigate the real material before it quietly hardens into scenes. For an adaptation, read the agreed source and identify gaps before choosing fidelity and departures.

**Decision:** What is supported, disputed, inferred or invented, and what are we promising to preserve or change?

**Record:** Source and claim ledger, factual chronology, uncertainty/departure notes and source-to-scene choices.

**Check:** A bibliography is not enough: each important source must support the particular claim. Permission and legal clearance remain distinct from factual plausibility.

### Challenge the premise and scale

**Purpose:** Test the central pursuit, opposition, relationship and ending hypothesis before writing many pages.

**Decision:** Is the strongest execution contained or expansive? What is the film’s promise to its audience?

**Record:** A premise review and story bible describing the movie being built.

**Check:** A premise can sound elegant while having no sustained action. Ask what the lead actually does when the first attempt fails.

### Design characters and causal scenes

**Purpose:** Turn intentions into situations, tactics, refusals and consequences. Choose the structure this movie needs.

**Decision:** Which choices change another character’s options, and what cannot return to its opening state?

**Record:** Character wants, outline, scene map, chronology and setup/payoff notes.

**Check:** The middle must develop the problem, not collect several versions of the opening situation.

### Write the actual screenplay

**Purpose:** Create complete filmable scenes with action and dialogue, in coherent groups that can be saved and resumed.

**Decision:** How does the story express itself through behavior, image, sound, timing and speech?

**Record:** Real screenplay sources and an exact progress record; an outline is not a finished draft.

**Check:** No omitted conversations, “continue similarly,” or treatment paragraphs standing in for pages.

### Read, diagnose and revise

**Purpose:** Read the complete current draft. Rank consequential weaknesses, protect strengths and repair causes before polishing lines.

**Decision:** Which intervention changes the dramatic experience enough to justify its cost and downstream consequences?

**Record:** Scene-specific findings, accepted repairs, revision log and updated story records.

**Check:** Show the before/after effect. More edits, pages or praise do not prove improvement.

### Check quality and factual promises

**Purpose:** Reassess agency, causality, voices, continuity, ending and the Bechdel result. Source-based work also gets a current truth/adaptation audit.

**Decision:** Which issues block the agreed goal, which remain uncertain, and which tradeoffs did you actually accept?

**Record:** Current-version reports, evidence, limits and unresolved findings.

**Check:** An old approval does not silently survive a changed script, source promise or audience claim.

### Derive the reader’s materials

**Purpose:** Write only the selected synopsis, package and pitches from the revised screenplay.

**Decision:** Who will read this, and what decision should the document help them make?

**Record:** Selected materials plus a cross-check against actual scenes, roles and ending.

**Check:** The package must not sell a more decisive protagonist, different ending or imaginary attachments.

### Build, inspect and hand off

**Purpose:** Compile actual PDFs, inspect every delivered page, measure length and collect editable sources with remaining concerns.

**Decision:** Are these the requested files, and are they ready for the specific next conversation?

**Record:** PDFs, page counts, visual findings, source/version records and a delivery inventory.

**Check:** A successful build does not prove good writing. A quality report does not prove readable layout. Both matter.

## Make the scene change what comes next

Atmosphere can be powerful. It becomes drama when someone’s behavior changes another person’s options.

For a major scene, name the immediate want, the obstacle, the tactic, the turn and the consequence. “She feels trapped” describes a condition. “She hides the key so the other worker cannot close the terminal” is an action with consequences. Quiet characters can act through withholding, consenting, refusing, leaving, protecting or surrendering something.

### An argument that becomes an event

*ILLUSTRATIVE CRAFT EXAMPLE*

**Before:** Two sisters explain that one always leaves and the other always stays. They reach no new decision, then repeat the grievance in a later scene.

**Intervention:** They have enough money to pay either a final crew shift or a repair deposit. The sister who wants to leave signs the crew payment in her own name, making tomorrow’s repair impossible without a new bargain.

**Why this changes the story:** Resentment is carried by a choice. The scene changes money, responsibility and the next problem. This is an invented teaching example, not advice about ferry operations or a prescribed plot.

- Give supporting characters independent wants and a boundary they can enforce. Someone who refuses the protagonist should be able to alter the plan, not merely articulate the theme.
- Differentiate relationships by offers and refusals. Three people should not all admire, understand and then reject the lead in the same way.
- Make a costly alternative genuinely available. Losing something you never could keep is harm, but it is not necessarily a chosen sacrifice.
- Let the ending spend value established earlier. A last-minute rule, confession or lucky document should not do the work of earlier choices.

<details>
<summary>Go further: voice, dry wit and cinematic behavior</summary>

A voice is not just vocabulary. It is what a person notices, avoids, insists on and does to get a response. One character corrects labels; another changes the subject with a practical task; another answers the question they wish had been asked. If everyone delivers the same polished insight, changing slang will not solve the underlying sameness.

For dry humor, test the action and timing before adding a clever line. A character can carefully straighten a form while refusing its premise. Humor may come from the gap between a rule and the person trying to obey it. Let some people be literal, tired, petty or unfunny; an ensemble of aphorists often sounds like one author.

Replace interpretive action with playable evidence. “He finally understands the cost” asks the camera to photograph an interpretation. A more specific beat might show him folding the unsigned offer and asking for the bus fare he now cannot afford. The right action belongs to this story; it is not a reusable symbol to paste into every script.

</details>

## Diagnose the cause before polishing the symptom

A useful review tells you what is wrong, where it happens, why it matters, and what a repair would change.

A broad note such as “the protagonist needs more agency” is a beginning, not a revision plan. It becomes usable when it identifies the scenes where other people make every consequential decision, the option the lead could control, and the later outcome that must change. The assistant should preserve what already works while making the smallest consequential intervention.

| Order | Question | Typical repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure first | What pursuit, choice or causal link is missing? | Rebuild a sequence, make a genuine alternative available, move a consequence earlier, or change who controls a decision. |
| Relationships and scene work | Do people want different things and use distinct tactics? | Give a supporter a conflicting goal, earn a renewed partnership through terms, or make a refusal change the plan. |
| Voice, action and rhythm | Is the scene playable and specific? | Replace explanatory dialogue with behavior, vary tactics/rhythm, compress repeated beats and remove decorative echoes. |
| Cross-document and layout finish | Does the package describe this version, and can a reader use the files? | Update synopsis and pitches; fix page breaks, isolated cues, overflow and one-page pitch length. |

Each repair should leave a record: finding, affected scenes, intervention, observed result and remaining limit. After changing a sequence, reread its setup, neighboring scenes and later payoff. A new decision can quietly break an earlier promise, a clock, a prop’s ownership or the synopsis.

```text
/story-revise Prioritize the two structural findings. Protect the ending image and the quiet tone. For each repair, show which scenes change and what consequence is different. Recheck downstream continuity before polishing dialogue. Do not restart the whole script.
```

<details>
<summary>When to stop, and when another full reading is necessary</summary>

Bounded review protects the work and the allowance. The default is a complete diagnosis followed by targeted repair/review, with unresolved consequential issues saved after the agreed rounds. It is not permission to declare READY after an arbitrary count.

A local line change may need only a local and downstream check. A changed protagonist goal, timeline, major relationship or ending can invalidate the earlier global reading and require a fresh complete read. Do not send the whole project through every available model merely to collect consensus. A specific unresolved concern should justify the next pass.

</details>

## Use studio mode to make decisions, not surrender the draft

An existing script enters through reading and diagnosis. It should not be forced through a fresh-idea interview or quietly rewritten into the template’s preferred film.

Ask the assistant to reconstruct the movie it actually read: pursuit, characters, key choices, structure and tone. Name what must be protected before proposing changes. Good notes distinguish a structural blocker from a taste choice, a production option and an unresolved factual question.

Claude Code

```text
/story-studio Read my complete draft before proposing changes. Protect the ending and restrained humor. Give me the most consequential notes as choices, with scene evidence, the proposed benefit and what I would lose. Ask before rewriting the identity of the film.
```

Codex

```text
$story-studio Read my complete draft before proposing changes. Protect the ending and restrained humor. Give me the most consequential notes as choices, with scene evidence, the proposed benefit and what I would lose. Ask before rewriting the identity of the film.
```

### A useful development question

*ILLUSTRATIVE CRAFT EXAMPLE*

**Before:** “The ending is too quiet. I will add a confrontation.” This silently substitutes the editor’s taste for the writer’s design.

**Intervention:** “The lead never answers the central question in the final sequence. Would you like a visible refusal in the existing scene, a direct confrontation that changes the tone, or to retain the ambiguity and accept that risk?”

**Why this changes the story:** The problem, alternatives and costs are visible. You can accept, modify, defer or decline. This is an illustrative question, not an actual recorded user approval.

The decision record matters later. “Keep the ambiguity” should survive a new session; “defer until we see the revised middle” should remain unresolved. The assistant applies only accepted changes within the agreed scope and shows what the resulting draft still needs. It does not treat silence as approval.

## Adaptation and truth are two different decisions

One asks how material becomes cinema. The other asks what you are promising the audience actually happened.

| Starting material | Adaptation decision | Truth and permission questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Your own fictional novel | Which viewpoint, span, scenes and character combinations make the strongest film? What can become action instead of narration? | Record the ownership/permission position; do not present invented events as history. |
| A memoir or oral history | What testimony, gaps, chronology and private perspective can the film represent? | Distinguish recollection, corroborated records, disputed accounts and invention. Record source-use permission as known, unresolved or unsupported. |
| An article about real events | What movie can emerge from a source written to explain rather than dramatize? | Reading a public article is not permission certification. Trace factual claims and identify edition, translation or other source constraints when relevant. |
| A real event used as inspiration | Which thematic spark survives into a substantially invented story? | Explain the boundary. “Inspired by” cannot make invented allegations about real people become documented facts. |

First preserve the original and identify what was actually extracted and read. Scanned pages, missing images, unclear audio and incomplete imports remain visible. Agree the adaptation’s center: whose experience, what span of time, what must remain, and which changes require your decision. A source-to-scene map records omissions, composites, invented dialogue and deliberate departures.

For both based-on and inspired-by work, the research system records claims and sources, not just a bibliography. Based-on work keeps central documented events grounded; inspired-by work permits a looser invented story while making its departures clear. A source must support the particular claim attached to it. Several pages from the same institution are not automatically independent corroboration. Disputed, unverified, inferred and invented material should retain those labels all the way into the synopsis and pitches.

```text
/story-adapt Adapt the source file in this folder into a contained feature. Read the complete agreed source first. Preserve the final choice and central relationship. Ask before combining characters or changing chronology. Track omissions and invented dialogue, keep permission questions unresolved where unsupported, and carry the screenplay through revision and selected PDFs.
```

<details>
<summary>Go further: what belongs in the research record</summary>

- A source identity, usable location, access date and the specific claim it supports; preserve relevant local notes or snapshots where possible.
- A chronology separate from screen order, a relationship map and a list of unresolved contradictions.
- A claim status: supported, disputed, unverified, inference or invention. The actual toolkit uses “verified” for its source-supported category; it still does not mean a professional historian certified it.
- A scene trace from factual material to the screenplay, plus the truth promise shown to audiences.
- A final review of the finished script and audience-facing materials. An early concept review does not cover later invented scenes.

</details>

## Understand what each gate proves

The point is to expose weaknesses and stale evidence, not to attach an impressive score to a pile of files.

| Gate | What the assistant must show | What a pass cannot prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Complete-draft craft review | Actual reading coverage, specific scenes, ranked issues, protected strengths and meaningful repairs. | Audience response, professional endorsement or an absence of remaining weaknesses. |
| Bechdel assessment and agency | Named women, an actual exchange and its non-man subject; PASS, FAIL or UNCLEAR. Separately test independent wants and consequential decisions. | Feminism, dimensional characters or overall screenplay quality. An honest fail is allowed unless you chose a pass requirement. |
| Continuity and causal coherence | Consistent chronology, information, prop state, available choices, setups/payoffs and the effect of changes. | That every interpretation will agree or that invented procedures are jurisdictionally accurate. |
| Truth and adaptation | A current promise, evidence/claim record, departures, source-to-scene choices and a finished-draft audit where applicable. | Legal clearance, permission or independent historical corroboration where none exists. |
| Current-version evidence | Reports tied to the files and factual/audience promises actually reviewed; changes can make receipts stale. | That a declared review was competently performed. File checks cannot evaluate art or confirm someone genuinely looked. |
| Actual delivery | Selected real builds, measured page counts, every-page visual findings and matching editable sources. | Perfect proofreading, production readiness, financing or a finished film. |

### A Bechdel repair that does more than tick a box

*ILLUSTRATIVE CRAFT EXAMPLE*

**Before:** Two named women discuss only the male protagonist. Adding two unrelated lines about coffee may change a test result without giving either woman a life.

**Intervention:** Give one woman a concrete operational goal that conflicts with the other’s plan. Let their negotiation change who controls a resource or what can happen next.

**Why this changes the story:** A qualifying exchange can emerge from substantive agency. The conversation still has to meet the named-women criterion; the assessment and the dramatic judgment remain separate.

The assistant records reports against source fingerprints: identifiers of the version it checked. If you change the screenplay, source promise, relevant evidence or audience wording, an old receipt may no longer apply. That is useful friction. The right response is to recheck the affected work, not copy a previous READY into a new record.

## Choose materials around the reader’s decision

The complete package is available, but making more documents is not the same as helping a reader.

A full selection produces 18 PDFs: one screenplay, one assembled studio package containing all 15 selected sections, and 16 separate one-page pitches. A selected package can contain fewer sections. You can request only a screenplay, a synopsis or one pitch. Working research and review notes are evidence, not mandatory sales collateral.

| Situation | A sensible starting selection | The question it helps answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An actor wants to understand a role | Screenplay, character breakdowns, actor pitch and perhaps scene showcase. | What can I play, what changes, and what relationships give the role range? |
| A producer is assessing the project | Synopsis, treatment, tone, positioning, preliminary scope and producer pitch. | What is the film, who might it serve, and what must be developed or verified next? |
| A funder or financier discussion | Synopsis, scope/assumptions, appropriate pitch and unresolved risks. | What is known, what needs professional costing or eligibility review, and what is merely proposed? |
| You are still finding the movie | Brief, outline and perhaps a provisional synopsis. | Is there a coherent story worth drafting? Most sales documents can wait. |

| Taglines | Offer several short creative directions for a possible poster or campaign. | Try one line about the shift deadline and another about the cost of keeping a record, both faithful to the ending. | Skip if no visual campaign is planned or no line adds clarity. | Co-production Pitch | Identify possible creative contributions, locations, partners, and unresolved co-production qualification questions. | Suggest story-relevant locations in a second territory, then list rights, spend, tax, and treaty issues for professional review. | Skip without a genuine cross-border creative need or researched rules. Do not imply qualification, partner interest, or incentive availability. |
| Public Funder Pitch | Explain cultural value, access plans, public benefit, and eligibility questions for a specific funder. | Propose a captioned community screening and paid consultation, pending remit, eligibility, and an access budget. | If no funder or criteria are known, describe possibilities only. Do not claim entitlement, guaranteed support, or endorsement. |
| Talent Representative Pitch | Give a representative career fit, role scope, accurate package status, and one possible next step. | State that the role has a substantial decision scene and the script is available; disclose that no cast is attached. | Skip if no representative or suitable role is identified. Do not imply referral, relationship, offer, or actor commitment. |
| Exhibitor Pitch | Describe the theatrical experience, audience, and programming proposition for an exhibitor. | The terminal's accumulating sounds and ensemble tension could invite a post-screening conversation. | If theatrical intent, completed sound mix, or audience proposition is unknown, qualify it. Do not promise turnout or release. |
| Cultural Partnership Pitch | Suggest a relevant program and concrete mutual benefit for a cultural or community partner. | Offer an optional paid panel on workplace records, with the partner free to decide whether the fictional story serves its audience. | Skip without a real partner or mutual benefit. Do not imply endorsement, representation, access, or unpaid participation. |
| Press Pitch | Offer a truthful story angle, concise description, and verified production facts to press. | Frame how ordinary shift work becomes a moral deadline, without inventing a behind-the-scenes anecdote or review quote. | Skip if there is no real news hook or verified production fact. Do not invent a true-story basis, interview, endorsement, review, or release news. |
| Craft Department Pitch | Describe specific visual, sound, design, costume, or editorial work invited by the project. | Make prop continuity a story challenge: stamps and document versions change who can act, so each must remain consistent and legible. | Skip if no department-specific opportunity is clear or needs are unsettled. Do not promise budget, schedule, or final-cut control. |

</details>

The assistant records your selection, builds only those outputs, checks each pitch’s readable one-page length and inspects the assembled package. Unselected templates may stay blank. For named talent, companies, comparable titles or market claims, verify current facts or clearly label unresearched profiles; creative fit never establishes attachment or interest.

## What a consequential revision actually looked like

The Ledger Room offers a real development record: an independent AI reading, a focused repair, a limited reread and acknowledged remaining risks.

The original demonstration concerns an archivist, Clara, and a courier, Jory, preserving disputed housing-compensation records without overriding the households represented in them. The first draft had 54 scenes and a recorded 71-page build. The revised screenplay retains 54 scene IDs and measures 91 physical pages, including its title page. The expansion was intended to enact missing choices and encounters, not meet an arbitrary page target.

### IFR-02: make the sacrifice an available choice

*ACTUAL DEMONSTRATION · ORIGINAL READ + REVISION RECORD*

**Before:** The initial reader found that Jory’s permanent job offer depended on a clean sign-off Clara had already undermined. An offer withdrawn before the decisive moment risked making Jory’s “sacrifice” an alternative she could not actually choose.

**Intervention:** The revision distinguishes Jory’s carrier declaration from Clara’s premises certificate. NORMAL RETURN remains a real route to the employment letter, but closes Jory’s own disclosure incident. S48 lets her refuse that closure; S49 shows the offer lapse afterward.

**Why this changes the story:** The cost follows a decision Jory controls. It also preserves her earlier harmful disclosure and practical need for work, instead of recasting her as a flawless moral voice.

### IFR-03: let a stakeholder refuse on screen

*ACTUAL DEMONSTRATION · SCENE-SPECIFIC REPAIR*

**Before:** Affected households were often described through Bea’s reports. Their objection to preservation stayed mediated by someone who wanted the project to proceed.

**Intervention:** S22 adds Ina’s direct refusal: her rent-payer account is not Bea’s to submit. Later packets respect the limit. A downstream check also changes S40’s informal note so it no longer quietly tells the story Ina withheld.

**Why this changes the story:** The encounter changes the material the protagonists are allowed to carry. The downstream repair shows why a strong new scene can still create a continuity or consent regression elsewhere.

### IFR-04 and IFR-05: earn trust and honor the clock

*ACTUAL DEMONSTRATION · TARGETED VERIFICATION*

**Before:** The partnership resumed quickly after a privacy breach. The climax also appeared to miss its own stated custody deadline.

**Intervention:** The characters negotiate time, pay, disclosure approval and a right to stop; later scenes test those terms. The revised clock distinguishes counter presentation from later checking, with the parent checking the specified deadline/seal chain.

**Why this changes the story:** A repair can change the relationship and procedural logic without adding a speech about trust or disguising the deadline. The scope of that reread remains explicitly limited.

The final package has 20 pages and all 16 one-page pitches. The author inspected every delivered PDF page through contact sheets, with selected full-resolution checks; this was layout review, not a word-by-word proofread. The independent full reading covered the frozen first draft; the later reading was a specified subset, not blanket independent final-draft certification. Shared polished procedural dialogue remains a stated risk, and no performed runtime, human table read or professional production validation is claimed.

## Carry a long project across finite sessions

Reasoning ability and active text capacity are different. A visible chat history need not all remain available to the model at once.

One draft might fit in a model’s context window. Add research, several versions, notes, tool results, the package and many rounds of revision, and the working set keeps growing. Models and apps have different input/output limits and compaction behavior. A giant prompt can be cut short; a recap can lose a detail that later matters. Larger windows help, but do not make exact recall unlimited.

Story Factory keeps a durable manuscript, story bible, scene map, approved decisions, source record and review evidence. The assistant reads what the current task needs, saves actual changes and records the next action. It does not reload every old draft and transcript into every request.

| Durable record | Why it matters next session |
| --- | --- |
| The manuscript and scene map | Identify the actual current writing, stable scene IDs, sequence progress and what remains unwritten. |
| Story bible and approved decisions | Protect the ending, character canon, form, constraints and changes you accepted or rejected. |
| Source ledger and adaptation record | Recover what supports a claim, what was invented, what was omitted and what permission/fidelity questions remain. |
| Reading/revision/quality records | Distinguish an original full read from a targeted reread, retain unresolved findings, and identify the version assessed. |
| Status and exact checkpoint | Name the actual next incomplete or stale stage, blocker and concrete next file/scene/action. |

## Use more control where it earns its cost

The beginner default is one coordinating assistant. Teams, model routing and optional memory are tools for specific problems, not requirements for a serious result.

A bounded helper can research one aspect of an event, audit continuity, read the draft from a fresh editorial posture or compare pitches with the screenplay. Parallel work is useful when the assignments are independent. Several writers changing the same script at once usually creates reconciliation work rather than a coherent movie.

| Task | A sensible boundary | What the lead must do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Historical context and chronology | Separate bounded source questions, explicit source limits and a shared truth promise. | Reconcile conflicts, check what each source supports and preserve uncertainty. |
| Independent quality reading | A defined current draft or clearly labeled excerpt; findings with scene evidence. | Compare the diagnosis with the actual script and decide what to repair. Same-model readers are not human experts. |
| Continuity or package alignment | Specific sequences, state variables or selected documents. | Check consequences outside the sample before making a whole-film claim. |
| A small line edit | Keep it with the coordinating author. | Avoid paying context/setup cost for a team whose work is smaller than its coordination. |

Claude Code

```text
/story-run Keep helpers off by default. If a specific research or review task would benefit, ask me with a bounded plan: at most two helpers, one round, no nested teams, no simultaneous screenplay edits and no new paid service. Otherwise work sequentially.
```

Codex

```text
$story-run Keep helpers off by default. If a specific research or review task would benefit, ask me with a bounded plan: at most two helpers, one round, no nested teams, no simultaneous screenplay edits and no new paid service. Otherwise work sequentially.
```

Off, ask and bounded auto modes are available. Actual capability, approval policy, plan capacity and remaining allowance vary by host and account. The controls validate plans and recorded activity; they cannot intercept every native tool or enforce a dollar budget the provider does not expose.

<details>
<summary>Go further: model choice, allowance and optional memory</summary>

Use economical models for bounded extraction, catalog comparisons and checks whose results the lead can verify. Reserve stronger editorial judgment for difficult structure, tone and conflicting creative decisions. A cheaper response that needs repeated repair can cost more attention than one well-scoped pass. No fixed token or dollar claim applies to every feature.

Save exact progress before allowance runs out. Resume after a reset rather than regenerating the draft. A new model or a new account is not automatically a solution to an unclear assignment. Token counters, cached input, subscription allowance and actual charges are different measures; unavailable usage is unknown.

GBrain was investigated through local lifecycle, native recall and bounded shared-service tests. Some native fidelity/write gates failed or were blocked, so an automatic memory adapter is not enabled. The default project-folder workflow remains sufficient. Do not give an optional memory system entire private scripts or unrelated productions by default.

</details>

## Decide what the next finished version is for

A local development handoff can be complete while many production and business questions remain open.

| Next conversation | Useful preparation | What remains separate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A trusted reader | Current screenplay, clear questions about the pursuit/relationships/ending, and an honest account of what changed. | A reader’s response is new evidence; it may reopen creative work even after a local READY judgment. |
| A performance or table reading | Playable scenes and specific questions about rhythm, clarity, voice and duration. | Printed page count does not establish performed runtime or how dialogue sounds aloud. |
| A director or craft discussion | Script, tone/visual world, selected scene showcase and the decisions still open to collaboration. | The package proposes a film; it does not attach people, secure locations or prove a production method. |
| A production feasibility discussion | Current script, proposed scope, assumptions and unverified cost drivers. | A real schedule, line budget, practical access, factual/jurisdictional checks and qualified rights review are not certified by the toolkit. |
| A tailored submission or meeting | Only the requested materials, accurate ending and status, checked PDFs and the reader’s exact limits. | Story Factory does not send, submit, negotiate or contact anyone merely because it produced a package. |

Keep editable sources, the selected PDFs, actual page counts, current review records and remaining concerns together. A recipient may need only one PDF; the whole folder need not travel with it. Private originals and raw research are not automatically part of the outward-facing handoff.

The assistant should be able to say: “This version is ready for this next use, these checks actually ran, and these limitations remain.” That is more useful than an unconditional claim that the movie is finished.
